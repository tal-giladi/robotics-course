## What you will learn

- Size a **servo flap** from the force budget: required edge force → pivot torque → servo selection with a margin, and why the SG90 fails the 0.15 kg payload.
- Design a **solenoid pin release**: the V² force law at real pack voltages, the net force after spring and friction, and the pulse width that releases without cooking the coil.
- Build the **zero-electronics pin + spring** release that fails open when the power dies mid-demo.
- Wire the weapon-domain servos correctly, including the **5 V tap amendment** the BOM's 12 V bus needs.
- Release a payload 20 times, log every release, and know why the ones that failed failed.

## Why it matters

The drop bin is the first weapon you will bolt to the rail, and it is the one that teaches
the whole pattern of module 21: a force budget before a part is bought, a mechanism that
works at the *worst* pack voltage, and a release you can confirm with a sensor. Get the
flap torque wrong and the stink bomb stays in the slot; get the pulse width wrong and the
solenoid is a heater; get the wiring wrong and the servo runs on a rail the e-stop does not
stop. All three are arithmetic, and this lesson does the arithmetic before you do the
welding (bolting).

## Concept

A static releaser has one job: **let go**. The payload is already in place; gravity (or the
audience's patience) is the rest of the mechanism. So the design question is not "how do I
move the payload" but "how do I *stop holding it* with margin at the worst moment".

Four mechanisms, cheapest first:

1. **Gravity drop bin with a servo flap** — the MG996R holds the flap shut; on command it
   swings open and the payload falls over the front caster. The servo is a *latch*, not a
   driver: it only has to resist the payload's weight plus friction, then give way.
2. **Servo latch** — same torque budget, different geometry: the servo tab holds a hook or
   a clamp. More parts, more places to jam; you choose it when the flap geometry doesn't fit.
3. **Solenoid pin release** — a 12 V solenoid pushes a 2 mm steel pin out of the payload's
   printed sleeve; the pin is held in by a torsion spring. The pulse is 100-300 ms; the
   solenoid is off 99% of the time. This is the *precise* release: it works on payloads the
   flap cannot (sealed sleeves, one specific slot, a payload that must not fall until the
   exact moment).
4. **Pin + torsion spring, zero electronics** — the spring holds the pin, a servo tab or the
   solenoid withdraws it; and if the power dies with the spring wound, the payload *still
   drops*. Fails open. Nothing in your demo can be more forgiving than a spring.

> [!TIP]
> **Ask your teacher:** "The solenoid has 20 N of net force at 9.9 V and the pin only needs
> 8 N. Why does the BOM offer an 85 N version at 2× the price?" (Answer in Level 3: margin
> is not for the nominal pin — it is for the corroded, un-lubricated, slightly-bent pin of
> shot 37, at empty pack, in a hot room.)

## Technical explanation

### Level 1 — the force budget

Before any part: what is *holding* the payload, and how much force releases it?

```text
holding forces (what you must beat):
  gravity component on the flap:   m·g·(leverage)     — 0.15 kg × 9.81 = 1.47 N at the edge
  friction in the slot:            ~0.5-1 N          (estimate, printed-on-printed)
  torsion spring on the pin:       5 N               (estimate, wound to spec)
  pin-in-sleeve friction:          3 N               (estimate, unlubricated steel)

release force = (holding) × safety 3
```

The safety factor is 3, not 1.5: the holding terms are estimates with unknown errors, the
payload masses vary (a "100 ml" stink bomb is 96-108 ml), and the mechanism is not a
precision device. A release that works at ×1.5 works *sometimes* — and "sometimes" is a jam
waiting for its demo.

### Level 2 — the servo flap (run the model)

```bash
py 21-war-machine/code/releaser.py flap
```

```text
drop-bin flap sizing (span 80 mm)
  heaviest payload          0.15 kg  (1.47 N weight)
  required edge force (x3)      4.41 N
  required pivot torque            0.353 N*m
  MG996R available torque          0.980 N*m  -> margin 2.77x
  SG90  available torque           0.180 N*m  -> margin 0.51x  TOO WEAK
```

The arithmetic: the flap is a lever, span 80 mm (the print dimension). The worst case is the
heaviest payload (0.15 kg) at the flap edge: the edge must push $F = m g \times 3 =
0.15 \times 9.81 \times 3 = 4.41$ N, and the pivot must resist $T = F \times \text{span} =
4.41 \times 0.08 = 0.353$ N·m. The MG996R's 10 kg·cm = 0.98 N·m (datasheet) leaves a 2.77×
margin; the SG90's 0.18 N·m does not even reach the requirement — which is exactly why the
BOM says "SG90 micro (0.15 N·m — fine for a light flap, too weak for a latch)".

**The 5 V tap amendment (spec delta, row 11).** The BOM §F wires "MG996R latches +
slingshot" straight to the 12 V weapon bus. The MG996R's voltage range is 4.8-7.4 V — it
cannot see 12 V. Amendment: one 2 A 12 V→5 V buck (LM2596-class module, ~₪20, 4Project or
Hackstore) on the weapon bus feeds all weapon-domain servos. The buck's input is *behind*
the e-stop relay, so the servos still die with the bus — the domain rule holds. Record this
row in your spec delta (21.01 Level 5): the course robot's servo power came from the Pi's
5 V rail; the war machine's weapon servos get a 5 V tap on the weapon bus.

Wiring (pin → pin; the Pico 2's free pins after karmel.yaml's allocations):

| From | To | Wire | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weapon bus 5 V (new buck, out +) | MG996R V+ (red) | red 24 AWG | 2 A branch, its own 2 A fuse |
| Weapon bus 5 V (out −) | MG996R GND (brown) | brown 24 AWG | common with the Pi's GND at the bus star |
| Pico 2 GPIO 8 | MG996R signal (orange) | yellow 24 AWG | PWM, 50 Hz |
| Pico 2 GPIO 9 | 2nd MG996R signal | yellow 24 AWG | the second flap |

### Level 3 — the solenoid pin (run the model)

```bash
py 21-war-machine/code/releaser.py pin
py 21-war-machine/code/releaser.py pulse
```

```text
solenoid pin sizing (spring 5 N + friction 3 N = 8 N required)
  solenoid  @12.6 V  @12.0 V  @10.8 V   @9.9 V   @9.0 V
  42n         38.3 N    34.0 N    26.0 N    20.6 N    15.6 N
  85n         85.7 N    77.0 N    60.9 N    49.9 N    39.8 N

  force at 9.9 V is 68% of rated (V^2 law) - the empty-pack case is the design case.
  both solenoids clear the required 8 N at every pack voltage; the 85 N buys margin
  for a corroded or un-lubricated pin. Pulse width: see `pulse`.

42n    pulse   125 ms  (travel 15 mm at 200 mm/s + 50 ms margin)
       heat per pulse ~ 0.50 J  - negligible at 1 pulse / 3 s duty
85n    pulse   125 ms  (travel 15 mm at 200 mm/s + 50 ms margin)
       heat per pulse ~ 0.88 J  - negligible at 1 pulse / 3 s duty

clamped to 100..300 ms: shorter can't finish the stroke,
longer just heats the coil. Add a flyback diode across the coil (21.03 wiring).
```

The V² law of 21.01 Level 2 in force form: a solenoid's force scales as $(V/V_{rated})^2$,
so at the empty-pack 9.9 V the 42 N solenoid has $42 \times (9.9/12)^2 = 28.6$ N *gross*;
subtract the spring (5 N) and the friction (3 N) and 20.6 N remain. The design case is the
empty pack, not the full one — the robot that fires at 100% battery is the easy 99% of
demos.

The pulse: the pin travels 15 mm at ~200 mm/s while powered (estimate — measure yours) = 75
ms, plus a 50 ms margin = **125 ms**, clamped to 100-300 ms. A solenoid left energised is a
3-5 W heater (the 42 N class); 0.5 J per pulse at one pulse per 3 s is nothing. The pulse is
the design: the `Weapon` FSM of 21.01 fires the solenoid for exactly its pulse width, then
waits for the release confirmation.

**The flyback diode.** The coil is an inductor: when the 12 V disappears in 1 µs, the coil
makes its own voltage — hundreds of volts, opposite polarity — hunting for a path. The path
is a diode across the coil, cathode to the negative rail (1N4007 or SS34): the spike
circulates through the diode instead of through your Pico's GPIO or your scope. Without it,
the first 20 pulses are fine and the 21st takes out something you did not expect (FE.18).

Wiring:

| From | To | Wire | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 V boost tap (out +) | solenoid coil + | red 18 AWG | 2 A branch, its own 2 A fuse |
| 12 V boost tap (out −) | solenoid coil − | black 18 AWG | diode across the coil, cathode to − |
| Pico 2 GPIO (via NPN or MOSFET switch) | coil drive | — | the Pico GPIO cannot sink the coil's ~1-2 A directly; a 2N2222 + base resistor, or an AO3400 MOSFET, does the switching — FE.12 |

### Level 4 — the pin + spring (zero electronics)

The torsion spring (5 N holding, wound to spec) holds the 2 mm × 40 mm pin in the sleeve.
Release options, in order of preference: a servo tab pushes the pin's tail (the servo is
already there for the flap — one MG996R can serve two functions: flap AND pin withdrawal);
or the solenoid. The spring's virtue is the **fail-open**: if the power dies with the spring
wound, the payload drops. In a demo, a dead robot with a payload that still releases is a
mystery; a dead robot with a payload that *doesn't* release is a failure. The pin+spring is
also the only release that works at 0 V, which is why the BOM gives it the "fails open" note
and the bench-test alt (a bent paper clip).

The two mechanisms share the bin: flaps on the drop slots, pin release on the sleeve slots.
Both are behind the e-stop relay (5 V tap for the servos, boost tap for the solenoids —
Level 2 and Level 3 wiring).

## Code

The sizing model ships with the module. Run all four commands:

```bash

py 21-war-machine/code/releaser.py pin      # solenoid force at every pack voltage

py 21-war-machine/code/releaser.py check    # all rules at once
```

The core of the model, as shipped in `code/releaser.py`:

```python
def flap_edge_force_n(payload_kg: float, safety: float = SAFETY_FACTOR) -> float:
    """Force at the flap edge needed to release a payload reliably: 3x its weight."""
    return payload_kg * G * safety


def flap_torque_n_m(edge_force_n: float, span_m: float = FLAP_SPAN_M) -> float:
    """Torque the flap pivot must resist: force at the edge x span (worst case, payload at edge)."""
    return edge_force_n * span_m


def solenoid_force_n(rated_n: float, v: float, rated_v: float = RATED_V) -> float:
    """The V^2 law from 21.01 Level 2: magnetic force scales with the square of the voltage."""
    return rated_n * (v / rated_v) ** 2


def solenoid_pulse_ms(stroke_m=PIN_STROKE_M, velocity_m_s=PIN_VELOCITY_M_S,
                      margin_ms=PIN_PULSE_MARGIN_MS, min_ms=PIN_PULSE_MIN_MS,
                      max_ms=PIN_PULSE_MAX_MS) -> float:
    travel_ms = stroke_m / velocity_m_s * 1000.0
    return min(max(travel_ms + margin_ms, min_ms), max_ms)
```

## Expected result

**21.03-E1.** Edge force: $0.05 \times 9.81 \times 3 = 1.47$ N. Torque: $1.47 \times 0.06 =
0.088$ N·m. SG90 margin: $0.18/0.088 = 2.04×$ — it works *for this payload*. But the SG90's
margin on the 0.15 kg payload is 0.51× (Level 2): a single servo type in a mixed-rack bin
is a maintenance bug, and the 0.15 kg slot is the one that jams first. Decision: MG996R
throughout; the SG90 buys ₪70 and sells you a mixed-parts bin. (If you defend the SG90 with
"a second bin for the light payloads", that is also correct — state the cost.)

**21.03-E2.** The checker passes for: 2 mm stroke → 100 ms (clamped up), 15 mm → 125 ms, 40 mm →
250 ms, 100 mm → 300 ms (clamped down). Your function must match `releaser.py`'s reference
to < 0.1 ms on all four.

**21.03-E3.** 40 releases. Expectations: the clay ball releases cleanly on ≥ 18/20 (a clean
release is the flap fully open — the slow-mo shows the ball clearing the slot in < 200 ms

mechanism (clearance, static, flap not fully open) — a jam without a mechanism is a
measurement you did not take.

## Troubleshooting

### Symptom: the flap doesn't open

Most likely first: the 5 V tap is sagging under the servo's stall current (measure it at
the servo plug while the flap is held shut — if it is below 4.5 V, the buck's input is
sagging too: add a 470 µF capacitor at the buck's input, 21.08 Level 3). Then: wiring (the
signal wire on GND — the servo spins to one end and stalls). Then: torque (you have the
SG90 in the 0.15 kg slot — Level 2).

### Symptom: the flap opens, the payload sticks

Clearance: the print shrank, the slot is 0.5 mm too small, the payload is wider than the
slot at one corner (balloons are ellipsoids — chamfer the slot 45°). Then: the flap opens
to 80° but the payload's bottom is still on the slot floor (the flap's travel is limited by
the bin wall — increase the travel to 100° or lower the front lip). Then: static (the
balloon — gel alt).

### Symptom: the pin half-ejects (8 mm of 15 mm)

The V² case at empty pack — but first: friction (lubricate the pin with a drop of oil; a
corroded pin doubles the 3 N term). Then: the pulse is too short (your pin is slower than
the 200 mm/s estimate — raise the pulse to 200 ms and re-test; 21.03-E2's clamp allows it).
Then: the spring is over-wound (5 N became 8 N — rewind one turn).

### Symptom: the solenoid stays warm after the pulse

The pulse is longer than you think (the FSM is holding the coil, not pulsing it — check the
release-confirmation path: if the confirmation never arrives, 21.01's FSM keeps FIRED and
some naive implementations re-pulse). Or: the flyback diode is reversed (the coil sees the
full switching spike every pulse and the driver fights it). Or: you are holding the coil
"to make sure" — the pulse is the design.

## Common mistakes

- Sizing the servo at full battery and testing at empty pack (the V² law is not only for
  solenoids — the MG996R's torque also drops ~25% at 9 V, and its stall current rises).
- Wiring the weapon servo to the Pi's 5 V rail and calling it a bench test — the bench test
  becomes the build when the e-stop is pressed and the servo keeps holding.
- Forgetting the flyback diode until the 21st pulse (FE.18: the inductor was patient).
- Using the solenoid's *rated* force in the budget instead of the *net* force after spring
  and friction (the 42 N number is a gross number; the budget runs on 20.6 N at 9.9 V).
- A flap whose travel ends with the payload still on the slot floor (travel is a dimension,
  put it in the print).

## Knowledge check

1. Why is the safety factor on the release force 3, and not 1.5 "because the numbers are
   close"?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Because every holding term is an estimate with unknown error: the payload mass varies
   (±5%), the friction depends on the print surface (±100%), the spring force depends on
   how hard it was wound (±20%). ×3 on a sum of estimates covers the joint error; ×1.5
   covers the mean, and the mean is what a bench test at 100% battery measures. The factor
   is the difference between "works in the demo" and "works in the demo *and* in shot 37".
   </details>

2. The MG996R is rated 0.98 N·m at 6 V. The flap needs 0.353 N·m. The margin is 2.77×.
   At 9.9 V on the (hypothetical) raw rail, what happens to the margin?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Nothing good: 9.9 V is above the MG996R's 7.4 V max, so the "margin" becomes a
   reliability question (the servo's internal regulator overheats, the lifetime drops).
   This is the 5 V tap amendment in one sentence — the margin is only real at the voltage
   the servo is actually fed, and on the weapon bus that voltage is made by the buck, not
   by the pack.
   </details>

3. Your pin release works at 12.6 V and fails at 9.9 V with the 42 N solenoid, but the
   model says 20.6 N net at 9.9 V against 8 N required. Where is the model wrong?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   The model's holding terms are estimates: the spring is over-wound (5→8 N) or the pin
   is corroded (3→8 N), and 8+8 = 16 N < 20.6 N still passes... so the model is *not*
   wrong on force — check the pulse: at 9.9 V the solenoid's *velocity* is also lower
   (force is lower, acceleration is lower), and the 125 ms pulse no longer finishes the
   15 mm travel. The fix is the pulse width at the low voltage (200 ms), not a bigger
   solenoid. The V² law hits the force AND the speed.
   </details>

4. What is the fail-open argument for the pin+spring, in one sentence, and which demo
   failure does it specifically prevent?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   "If the power dies with the spring wound, the payload still drops." It prevents the
   worst demo failure: the robot dead mid-sequence with a payload still latched in the
   slot — the audience sees a machine that holds its breath and never exhales. With the
   spring, the dead robot at least *lets go*.
   </details>

5. The flyback diode is across the coil, cathode to the negative rail. You wire it
   reversed. What do you see on pulse 1, and what do you see on pulse 21?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Pulse 1: nothing visible (the diode blocks the normal freewheeling path, the spike
   goes through the MOSFET's body diode — it survives). Pulses 2-20: the MOSFET and the
   coil's insulation are slowly stressed by the full spike every time (the body diode was
   never rated for 100 V at 50 A of inductive current). Pulse 21: something opens — the
   MOSFET, the coil's first turn, or the Pico's GPIO that is driving the base. The
   reversed diode is a *delayed* failure, which is worse than a broken one.
   </details>
