## What you will learn

- Extend the CG and tipping model of 21.01 to a **weapon that moves**: the throw arm swung forward shifts the centre of gravity, and the braking limit is what it eats.
- Model the pack as a **Thevenin source** (V_oc + R_internal + branch resistance) and compute the voltage the weapon bus actually sees at 15 A of launch current — at full charge and at empty pack.
- Explain the **interaction**: a servo's stall current rises as the voltage drops, so the sag is worst exactly when the launch is happening — and size the release-confirm timeout against the slow case.
- Run the **runtime tax**: the added mass costs minutes through the wheels, and the firing costs ~1% of the pack per 100 shots.
- Measure all of it with the INA219/INA226 and the scale, and replace the (estimate) numbers in the spec delta with your own.

## Why it matters

The weapons tax the robot through three budgets at once, and the budgets interact in a way
the BOM's part list does not show. The mass moves the centre of gravity (21.01's tipping
limits change); the peak current sags the voltage at the worst instant (the launch), and the
sag changes the behaviour of every actuator on the bus (the servo slows, the solenoid loses
force, the 5 V buck's input drops). A robot that works at 100% battery in the bench test is
a robot that has not met its worst case. This lesson builds the three models — CG with
moving weapons, Thevenin sag, runtime — runs them on the BOM's packages, and tells you which
number to measure when the model and the robot disagree.

## Concept

Three budgets, one interaction:

```text
    MASS  ──▶ CG and tipping limits (21.01's model, extended to moving weapons)
    CURRENT ─▶ voltage sag at the weapon bus (Thevenin: V = V_oc − I·R)
    INTERACTION: the sag happens DURING the launch, when the servos and
                 solenoids are working hardest — the servo's stall current
                 RISES as the voltage drops (I ≈ P/V), so the branch sees
                 more current exactly when you least want to blow the fuse.
```

The design consequences: the release-confirm timeout (2 s in the 21.01 FSM) must have
margin for the *slow* case (the servo at 8.95 V, not 12.6 V); the solenoids ride the 12 V
boost tap (21.01's answer to the V² law); and the runtime is a mass problem, not a firing
problem (firing costs ~1% of the pack per 100 shots — the wheels carry the tax).

> [!TIP]
> **Ask your teacher:** "The pack's internal resistance is 24 mΩ and the branch is 39 mΩ —
> why does the wire gauge (18 vs 16 AWG) barely matter?" (Answer in Level 2: at 0.6 m of
> wire, the copper is 4.3 vs 2.4 mΩ — a 1.9 mΩ difference against 63 mΩ total. The pack,
> the fuse, the relay and the connector are the resistance; the wire is the rounding error.
> Gauge matters when the wire is long, and the weapon bus's wire is not.)

## Technical explanation

### Level 1 — the CG with a weapon extended

21.01's model is a static sum: `cg_shift(base_mass, base_cgx, base_cgz, added)`. The
extension is a *moving* part: the throw arm rotates, and the CG moves with it. The Siege
package (6.46 kg, CG at x = +53 mm, z = 65 mm — `war_power_budget.py mass`) carries the AGF
arm (0.9 kg, folded at (0.08, 0.08)). Swing the arm to 45° forward: the arm's mass centre
moves to (0.08 + 0.25, 0.08 + 0.17) = (0.33, 0.25).

```python
from war_power_budget import cg_shift

# Siege with the AGF arm swung 45° forward (the arm's own CG moves with it)
base_mass, base_x, base_z = 6.46 - 0.9, 0.0, 0.0   # the rest of the Siege, re-derived
# (the clean way: subtract the folded arm, add the extended arm)
m, x, z = cg_shift(5.56, 0.0453, 0.0605, [(0.9, 0.33, 0.25)])
print(f"extended: total {m:.2f} kg  CG x {x*1000:+.0f} mm  z {z*1000:.0f} mm")
```

(The 5.56 kg / (45.3, 60.5) mm base is the Siege without the arm — run the `cg_shift`
against the part list in `war_power_budget.py` to get your exact numbers; the point is the
*delta*.) The result: the CG moves from (x = +53 mm, z = 65 mm) to roughly (x = +75 mm,
z = 82 mm) — forward and up. The tipping limits recompute:

- **Rear-tip limit** (accelerating forward, tipping over the front wheels… no — over the
  *front* axle edge, i.e. the robot rears up): $a = g \cdot x_{CG}/z_{CG}$ rises from 7.88
  to ~9.0 m/s² (the higher, more forward CG is *more* stable against rearing — the forward
  weight presses the front wheels down).
- **Braking limit** (decelerating, tipping nose-down over the front edge): $a = g \cdot
  (x_{caster} - x_{CG})/z_{CG}$ falls from 7.11 to ~4.5 m/s² — the braking margin is halved.

The design rule: **do not brake hard while the arm is extended forward**. The 1.0 m/s²
drive limit is nowhere near 4.5 m/s², so the normal case is safe; the rule exists for the
autonomous case (a Nav2 stop at 1.5 m/s² with the arm up is a 1/3-margin event). Record it
in the spec delta as a *software* limit, not a hardware one: `max_decel_arm_extended: 2.0`.

### Level 2 — the Thevenin sag (run the model)

The pack is a Thevenin source: an ideal voltage V_oc in series with its internal
resistance. Add the branch — fuse, relay, connector, wire — and the weapon bus sees:

$$V_{load} = V_{oc} - I \cdot (R_{int} + R_{branch})$$

```bash
py 21-war-machine/code/war_power_budget.py sag --current 15
```

```text
voltage sag at I = 15.0 A (pack -> weapon bus path)
  pack internal R        24 mohm   (3S 35E, estimate)
  branch R               39 mohm   (fuse 5 + relay 20 + connector 10 + 18 AWG 0.6 m = 4.3 mohm)
  total R                63 mohm
  at full charge  (12.6 V): sag  0.95 V   load sees 11.65 V
  at empty pack   (9.9 V): sag  0.95 V   load sees  8.95 V

  a 12 V solenoid on the RAW rail at empty pack: 56% of rated force (V^2)
  the same solenoid on the 12.0 V boost tap: 100% at every pack voltage -
  the boost buys back the V^2 loss; servos and the actuator just run a bit slower.
```

And the gauge question (the TIP above):

```bash
py 21-war-machine/code/war_power_budget.py sag --current 15 --awg 16
```

```text
  branch R               37 mohm   (fuse 5 + relay 20 + connector 10 + 16 AWG 0.6 m = 2.4 mohm)
  total R                61 mohm
  at full charge  (12.6 V): sag  0.92 V   load sees 11.68 V
  at empty pack   (9.9 V): sag  0.92 V   load sees  8.98 V
```

18 → 16 AWG saves 0.03 V out of a 0.95 V sag. The pack (24 mΩ) and the branch hardware
(35 mΩ of fuse + relay + connector) are the resistance; the copper is 4.3 mΩ of it. Buy
the 16 AWG if the branch is longer than a metre (the wire is now the majority of the
branch); at 0.6 m, buy the time you save.

The solenoid row is the V² law of 21.01 in budget form: a 12 V solenoid on the raw rail at
empty pack has $(8.95/12)^2 = 56\%$ of its rated force — the 42 N solenoid is a 23.5 N
solenoid at the worst moment. The 12.0 V boost tap is the fix: 100% at every pack voltage,
at the cost of a converter's quiescent current (~4 W continuous, BOM §A).

### Level 3 — the interaction: sag during the launch

The launch is the peak: the cannon's actuator draws ~5.5 A (21.04), the solenoid pin adds
~1.5 A for 125 ms, the slingshot servo adds ~2 A while it cocks — 7-9 A on the weapon bus
at the same instant, on top of the drive's duty. The sag at 9 A is 0.57 V (0.95 V at 15 A
scales linearly): the bus sees 12.03 V at full charge, 9.33 V at empty.

Now the actuator behaviours *at that voltage*:

- **The MG996R flap servo** (on the 5 V tap, whose *input* is the sagging bus): the buck's
  output tracks its input down to the dropout (~1.5 V below the 5 V out, i.e. the output
  holds to ~6.5 V input) — at 9.33 V input the 5 V tap still makes 5 V, and the servo is
  fine. *But* the buck's current limit is the branch's: a 2 A buck with the flap stalling
  draws 2 A from a sagging input, and the input sags *more* (the buck's input current is
  higher than its output current by the conversion loss). The fix is the 470 µF bulk
  capacitor at the buck's input (FE.15: the capacitor is the local energy store that decouples
  the buck from the branch's transient).
- **The servo's speed** drops with the voltage: the MG996R's slew is ~25% slower at 9 V
  than at 12 V (the internal regulator and the motor both see the lower rail). The flap
  that opens in 1.0 s at full battery opens in 1.3 s at empty. The release-confirm timeout
  is 2 s — the slow case has 0.7 s of margin. *That is the number the 21.01 FSM's 2 s was
  sized against*, and it is why the timeout must not be "tightened to 1 s" without
  re-running this level.
- **The solenoid** on the raw rail (if you skipped the boost tap): 56% force at empty pack
  (Level 2) — the pin half-ejects (21.03's troubleshooting, first row).
- **The 5 V compute rail**: the Pi's regulator is on the *always-on* domain (20.02), not
  the weapon bus — the launch does not brown out the Pi. The weapon bus's 5 V tap is the
  one that sags, and it feeds only the weapon servos. The domains are doing their job.

The design rules, in one place:

1. The solenoids ride the 12 V boost tap (V² law — 21.01).
2. The weapon servos ride the 5 V tap *with a 470 µF bulk cap at the tap's input* (Level 3).
3. The release-confirm timeout (2 s) is sized against the slow-servo case at empty pack —
   re-run it if you change the servo or the timeout.
4. The 30 A relay and the 5 A cannon fuse are the *peak* budget (21.01 spec delta, row 10)
   — the sag does not blow the fuse, but the stall current does, and the stall is what the
   sag causes.

The runtime tax is a *mass* problem: the flat-floor drive power is proportional to the mass
on the wheels (the 20.02 model), and 2.85 kg of weapon platform on the 2.66 kg base is a
+107% drive-power increase — 107 min to 78 min is the Assault's tax. The firing is a
rounding error: 100 cannon shots are 0.9% of the pack. Plan the demo for *distance and
time*, not for *shots* — the robot will run out of battery long before it runs out of
ammunition (which is the demo writer's problem, 21.10).

The sag core, as shipped in `code/war_power_budget.py`:

```python
PACK_R_INT_OHM = 0.024   # 3S 35E, ~8 mΩ per cell (datasheet), in series
FUSE_R_OHM = 0.005
RELAY_R_OHM = 0.020
CONNECTOR_R_OHM = 0.010
COPPER_R_PER_M = {18: 0.0071, 16: 0.0040, 14: 0.0025}   # Ω/m, datasheet


def branch_resistance_ohm(awg: int = 18, wire_length_m: float = 0.6) -> float:
    """Fuse + relay + connector + wiring: the whole path from pack to the weapon bus."""
    return (FUSE_R_OHM + RELAY_R_OHM + CONNECTOR_R_OHM
            + wire_resistance_ohm(awg, wire_length_m))


def voltage_at_load(v_oc: float, current_a: float, r_total_ohm: float) -> float:
    """Thevenin: what the load actually sees while the current flows."""
    return v_oc - current_a * r_total_ohm


def solenoid_force_fraction(v: float, rated_v: float = 12.0) -> float:
    """The V^2 law as a fraction of rated solenoid force (21.01 Level 2)."""
    return (v / rated_v) ** 2
```

## Troubleshooting

### Symptom: the servo stalls only when the pack is low

The sag, not the servo: the 5 V tap's input sags under the launch current, the tap's
current limit clips the servo's stall, and the servo stalls. The fix is the 470 µF bulk
capacitor at the tap's input (Level 3, rule 2) — the capacitor supplies the stall's first
100 ms from its own charge, and the tap recovers after. Measure it: the tap's input
voltage during the stall (if it dips below 6.5 V, the tap is in dropout; the cap is the
fix).

### Symptom: the runtime is 50 min, not the model's 78

The mass estimate was wrong (the (estimate) parts in `check`'s output — weigh them, 21.01
rule 5) or the drive duty is higher than the course's 17 W profile (the robot drives
faster/harder than the benchmark — the 20.02 profile is a *benchmark*, your demo is not the
benchmark). The mass is the usual culprit: 2.85 kg of (estimate) at x = +75 mm is the
number that moves the runtime, and the scale is cheaper than the model.

### Symptom: the Pi brown-outs during the launch

It should not (Level 3: the compute rail is on the always-on domain). If it does: the
always-on regulator's input is the *same* sagging branch (a wiring error — the 20.02
domain split was not made), or the 5 V regulator's input cap is missing (the regulator's
datasheet requires 10 µF minimum on the input — FE.15). The fix is the wiring, not the
regulator.

### Symptom: the model's sag is 0.95 V, the measured is 1.8 V

The branch is longer than 0.6 m (measure the wire — the XT60 runs are the usual hidden
metres), or the relay's contact resistance is the 50 mΩ of a tired relay, not the 20 mΩ
of the datasheet (the relay is a *wear* part — 21.09's FMEA row), or the pack is older
than the 35E's datasheet (Level 2's caveat — your R_int is the measured R_total minus the
branch, and it is probably 40-60 mΩ, not 24).

## Common mistakes

- Sizing the release-confirm timeout at full battery (the 2 s is the *slow-servo* number —
  Level 3 — tighten it and the empty-pack launch is a jam).
- Trusting the wire gauge at 0.6 m (the copper is 4.3 mΩ of a 63 mΩ branch — the TIP above;
  the gauge is the long-wire problem, not the weapon-bus problem).
- Putting the solenoids on the raw rail "to save the boost converter" (the 4 W quiescent is
  cheaper than the half-ejected pin of shot 37).
- Measuring the sag at 100% and calling the model validated (the empty pack is the design
  case — Level 2's second row is the one that matters).
- Forgetting that the runtime is a *mass* budget (the firing is 1% per 100 shots; the
  2.85 kg is the tax — plan the demo for distance, 21.10).

## Knowledge check

1. The pack is at 9.9 V, the launch draws 15 A, the total R is 63 mΩ. The 5 V tap's output
   is 4.8 V. Is the tap in dropout? (The dropout is ~1.5 V below the 5 V output.)
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   The tap's input sees 9.9 − 15×0.063 = 9.9 − 0.945 = 8.955 V. The dropout is at
   5 + 1.5 = 6.5 V input. 8.955 > 6.5 — the tap is NOT in dropout; it makes 5 V (and the
   4.8 V reading is the servo's load on the tap's output, which is fine — the 2 A branch
   has headroom). The tap fails at input < 6.5 V, which is a 15 A launch from a *deader*
   pack (9.9 V is the low warning, not the cutoff — 9.9 is still 24 mΩ × 15 A = 0.36 V
   below the 9.9, i.e. the input is 8.955, not 6.5). The dropout case is the 9.0 V cutoff
   at 20 A: 9.0 − 1.26 = 7.74 V — still above 6.5. The tap is robust; the *servo's* stall
   current is the part that clips (Level 3).
   </details>

2. Why is the firing cost (~1% per 100 shots) so small, and what does that imply for the
   demo's energy planning?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Because the weapons' energy is small (12 J per cannon shot = 3.3 mWh) against the
   pack's 37.8 Wh, and the demo's energy is the *drive's* (17 W average × the session
   time). The implication: plan for distance and time, not for shots — the robot runs out
   of battery at ~78 min (the Assault's runtime) regardless of how many payloads it has,
   and a 30-minute demo with 50 shots has fired 0.45% of the pack. The ammunition is not
   the constraint; the range's length is.
   </details>

3. The cannon's actuator stalls at 9 A (not the 5.5 A nominal). The 5 A fuse does not
   blow. Why, and is that safe?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   A 5 A blade fuse is a *time-current* device: it blows on sustained overcurrent (a 50%
   overcurrent, 7.5 A, in ~30-60 s; a 100% overcurrent, 10 A, in a few seconds), not on a
   100 ms spike. The stall is 100 ms — the fuse's thermal mass does not heat. That is the
   design: the stall is a *transient* the fuse must survive, and the relay's 30 A rating
   is the *peak* budget (21.01 spec delta row 10). It is safe until the stall becomes a
   *sustained* stall (the channel is jammed — the actuator holds 9 A for 5 s, and the fuse
   is now the only thing between the branch and the pack — the 21.09 FMEA row "fuse
   mis-blow" is the flip side: the fuse that should not blow at 9 A × 100 ms must blow at
   9 A × 5 s).
   </details>

4. The AGF arm is extended 45° forward. The robot brakes at 1.5 m/s² (an autonomous stop).
   The extended-arm braking limit is ~4.5 m/s². Is that safe, and what is the margin?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Safe: 1.5 / 4.5 = 1/3 of the limit — the same 3× safety factor the release forces use
   (21.03 Level 1). The margin is the 3×, and it is why the *normal* autonomous stop is
   fine with the arm up. The rule (Level 1's `max_decel_arm_extended = 2.0`) is the
   software limit that keeps the margin at 2.25× or better — a Nav2 stop at 2.5 m/s² with
   the arm up is a 1.8× event, and the 21.10 runbook's "arm down before the stop" step is
   the demo's version of the same rule.
   </details>

5. You measure the sag at 100% and it matches the model (0.95 V). At 30% it is 1.6 V. The
   model says 0.95 V at both. What changed, and which number goes in the spec delta?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   The pack's internal resistance: the 35E's 8 mΩ/cell is the *new-cell, 1C* datasheet
   value; a two-year-old pack at 30% SoC has a higher R_int (the SEI layer, the
   electrolyte, the cell's age). The measured R_total at 30% = (V_before − V_min)/I_peak
   is the number that matters for the *worst* launch (the empty pack is the design case —
   Level 2), and it goes in the spec delta as the measured R_int (the model's 24 mΩ is
   replaced by your measured value, and 21.10's runbook step 1 "pack V before" uses your
   number for the go/no-go). The model's *shape* (V = V_oc − I·R) is still right; the
   *parameter* is the one that aged.
   </details>

## Practical challenge

**The sag campaign.** Run the full measurement: (a) the pack's R_int at 100% and 30% (10
launches each, Level 2's method); (b) the branch's R (the 4-point method, or the model's
components measured one by one — the relay's contact resistance is the one you cannot
datasheet); (c) the 5 V tap's input and output during a launch (the dropout check, Q1);
(d) the runtime at the Assault's mass (the 20.02 method, your mass). Deliverable: the
spec delta with every (estimate) in the `mass` and `sag` outputs replaced by your
measurement, and the one-sentence verdict per row: the model was right / the model's
parameter aged / the model's shape was wrong.
