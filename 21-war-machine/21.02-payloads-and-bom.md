## What you will learn

- Classify any candidate payload into the four release classes — **drop, launch, spray, stick** — and explain why the release mechanism, not the payload's identity, is what determines which subsystem you buy.
- Read the module's bill of materials ([hardware/war-machine-bom.md](hardware/war-machine-bom.md)) as an engineer: exact components, Israeli shop links, prices with dates, and the "Compat" gotcha column.
- Compute the **impact energy** of every payload class at realistic velocities — the one number that tells you whether the thing you are "shooting" at a paper target or at a pet.
- Size the **payload rack**: mass budget, slot geometry, and the constraint that keeps the payloads out of the LiDAR plane and under the tipping limits.
- Design the **payload state machine**: how the robot knows a payload is loaded, spent, or jammed — and why "unverified" means "not armed".

## Why it matters

A weapon without a payload is an actuator; a payload without a weapon is a prop. The interesting
question in between is *which payload goes in which release mechanism*, and the answer is not
intuition, it is three numbers: the payload's mass, its size, and the energy it arrives with.
Get the mass wrong and the centre of gravity moves (21.08); get the size wrong and the payload
jams in its slot (21.09); get the energy wrong and your "kid-safe" bouncy ball is a 6 J object
hitting a standing person at 3 m.

This lesson is the shopping list and the physics of it. Every row of the BOM is something you
can buy over the counter in Israel without a licence or a customs form — paintballs at a
paintball shop, CO2 cartridges at a reef store, magnets at a caravan supplier — and the links
point at the shops that the course already verified.

## Concept

Remember the triple from 21.01: every weapon is **payload + releaser + trigger**. This lesson
owns the payload column, and the first move is a classification that looks backwards:

> Payloads are not classified by what they are. They are classified by **how they get
> released**. A stink bomb and a steel ball are both "things that get dropped". A glue stick
> and a confetti pouch are both "things that get squeezed". The release mechanism determines
> which subsystem you buy, which fuse it rides, and which state machine guards it.

That is why the BOM is organised the way it is — by release class, not by what the payload
is:

| Release class | What happens | Releaser (21.03/21.04) | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Drop** | gravity does the work; the payload falls from 6-10 cm | drop bin, servo flap, solenoid pin, pin+spring | [21.03](21.03-releasers-i-latches.md) |
| **Launch** | the weapon stores energy and gives it to the payload | slingshot, cannon, throw arm, CO2 magazine | [21.04](21.04-releasers-ii-launch.md) |
| **Spray** | a nozzle or a squeezed can does the work | aerosol can (drop it), airbrush (servo) | [21.03](21.03-releasers-i-latches.md) |
| **Stick** | the payload lands and *stays* — magnet, glue, hook | any drop/launch releaser + a sticky payload | [21.04](21.04-releasers-ii-launch.md) |

"Spray" and "stick" are payload *properties*, not new mechanisms: a stink bomb is a drop-class
payload that sprays on impact, a glue stick is a drop-class payload that sticks, a magnet is a
stick-class payload. The mechanism list is still two: **let go** (21.03) and **give energy**
(21.04).

> [!TIP]
> **Ask your teacher:** "Why is the stink bomb not a 'spray-class weapon' with its own
> releaser? Is the aerosol valve a mechanism?" (Answer in 21.03 Level 1: it is a payload with
> a *passive* release — the valve opens on impact or on a squeeze — and passive releases are
> cheaper than any mechanism you could bolt on.)

## Technical explanation

### Level 1 — the properties that matter

For every candidate payload, four numbers decide its fate:

1. **Mass** — it moves the CG (21.08) and it sets the impact energy. The BOM's rack budget is
   0.4-0.6 kg of drop-class payload on top of the rail; the Scout package carries 0.45 kg
   (three slots, ~0.15 kg each — the number that appears in `war_power_budget.py`).
2. **Size** — the slot must fit it with 0.5-1 mm clearance; too tight and it jams (friction
   beats gravity), too loose and it tilts and *then* jams. Payloads are printed sleeves or
   standard objects (balloon, BB in a paper-cup sleeve), never loose shapes.
3. **Fragility** — a balloon leaks in 20 minutes; a paintball cracks if dropped; a magnet
   sticks to the deck and becomes a phantom LiDAR obstacle (21.09 FMEA). The BOM's "Alt"
   column always has a less-fragile substitute.
4. **Energy on arrival** — the impact energy is the safety number (Level 2).

And one non-number: **OTC status**. Everything in this BOM is walk-in-purchasable in Israel
without a licence, as of the BOM's verification date (2026-09-23/24): paintballs and BBs are
sports items, the CYMA CO2 magazine is airsoft (the highest-energy weapon you can buy without
a licence), sparklers are holiday items. If a payload needs a licence (a real airgun, a CO2
rifle), it is not in this course — say so in the spec delta.

### Level 2 — impact energy: the safety number

The payload's energy on arrival is $E = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ for launch class and
$E = m g h$ for a pure drop. The numbers, with the realistic velocities of 21.04 (the
`projectile_range.py` model, not the BOM's optimistic "estimate" column):

| Payload | m | v at target | E on arrival | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paintball, dropped from the bin (h = 0.1 m) | 0.00025 kg | 1.4 m/s | 0.0005 J | a raindrop |
| Stink bomb, dropped (h = 0.1 m) | 0.15 kg | 1.4 m/s | 0.15 J | a coin dropped on your hand |
| Magnet 25 mm, thrown (AGF arm) | 0.04 kg | 2.1 m/s | 0.09 J | a soft toss |
| Steel ball 5 mm, slingshot | 0.004 kg | 17.4 m/s | 0.60 J | a firm pinch — **eye protection** |
| Bouncy ball 4 cm, cannon | 0.05 kg | 15.5 m/s | 6.0 J | a tennis serve, small ball |
| BB 0.25 g, CYMA CO2 | 0.00025 kg | 100 m/s | 1.25 J | a hard slap — **eye protection** |

Two lessons in this table. First, **mass and velocity are not interchangeable**: the bouncy
ball arrives with 10× the slingshot ball's energy because $v^2$ dominates, and it is the one
that needs a 25 m range clearance. Second, **the 6 J bouncy ball is the safety-critical
payload of the whole build** — it is the most energetic thing the Scout/Assault can throw, it
is soft, bouncy (it rebounds into places you did not aim) and it is what the audience
understands. The CO2 BB is *more* energetic per shot at 100 m/s but the CYMA magazine's real
muzzle velocity must be measured (21.04 Level 5: chronograph yours).

> [!NOTE]
> The BOM's launch-class energy table ("~4 J slingshot", "~12 J cannon", "~4 J AGF") lists
> *stored or rated* energies. The model in [21.04](21.04-releasers-ii-launch.md) converts them
> to projectile energy with efficiency factors — the slingshot's real number is 0.60 J, the
> cannon's 6.0 J, the AGF arm's 0.40 J. The model is the honest one; the BOM numbers are the
> store's.

### Level 3 — spray and stick: the payload as mechanism

**Spray.** The BOM's spray payloads are all *aerosol on impact*: the stink bomb (100 ml
can, ~0.15 kg), and in season the sparkler (light + hiss, ~0.01 kg). The release is passive —
the can is dropped, the impact or a servo plunger squeezes it. The engineering is in the
**slot**: a flame shield for the burning items (the BOM says so — the cold-smoke stick and the
sparkler each get their own shielded slot), and a drip tray for the stink bomb (aerosol finds
the floor). If you want *directed* spray (airbrush paint, glitter), that is a nozzle on a
servo — the same servo-flap mechanism of 21.03 with a canister instead of a flap; budget the
airbrush at ₪60-150 (estimate — verify at a hobby shop; not in the verified BOM) and treat the
paint as a consumable with its own slot.

**Stick.** The magnet (25×10 mm N52 disc, ~0.04 kg; 50×15 mm for the AGF heavy-throw, 0.15 kg)
and the UHU glue stick (50 g) are drop/launch payloads whose *payload* does the work after
impact: the magnet holds to any steel target, the glue holds to anything. Two gotchas: the
magnet is not ferrous *enough* to disturb the LiDAR's beam, but a magnet dropped on the deck
is a phantom obstacle the robot will drive into (21.09); the glue stick needs a *squeeze* —
either a servo plunger (21.03's pin-release with a flat tab) or you accept that it lands as a
dead stick and the audience squeezes it (demo value > payload value).

### Level 4 — the payload state machine

The robot must *know* about its payloads, or it will dry-fire (21.09). Each slot has a
verification: a weight switch under the slot (the payload's mass closes it), or an optical
gap sensor (the payload breaks the beam). The per-slot states:

```text
EMPTY ──payload placed, verified──▶ LOADED ──released──▶ SPENT
   ▲                                   │
   └──────────verified again───────────┘
                              └──not verified in 2 s──▶ JAMMED (payload somewhere)
```

The rule from 21.01's weapon FSM applies per slot: **LOADED is a verified fact, not a belief**
— a slot whose weight switch does not close is EMPTY, even if you watched the payload go in.
That one rule kills the dry-fire failure class: the weapon cannot be armed from a slot that is
not verified LOADED.

## Expected result

**21.02-E1.** (a) Glass bottle: drop class, but *fragility = 1/5* — the glass breaks on the first
hard landing; reject it, use the 100 ml plastic stand-in or the deodorant stick. (b) Paper
dart: launch class, too light for the solenoid pin (0.03 kg < the pin's 8 N requirement is
fine — the problem is the *slot*: a dart is a loose shape); needs a sleeve or reject. (c)
Steel ball 0.2 kg: launch class only — 0.2 kg × 1.4 m/s drop = 0.4 J is harmless, but at
slingshot velocity (17 m/s) it is 29 J — a rivet gun; not for the Scout, that is the Siege
cannon's payload and needs the 25 m clearance. (d) Sparkler: drop class with flame shield —
in season only. (e) Flour bag: spray class on impact — the *best* area-denial payload in the
list, 0.4 kg × 1.4 m/s = 0.4 J, OTC (grocery), and it makes the audience laugh. Reject (a)
and (c) from the Scout.

**21.02-E2.** Your layout will differ; the pass criteria are: total payload ≤ 0.6 kg; the 0.15 kg
payloads in the two lowest slots at x ≤ +0.08 m; the computed Scout CG at x = +38 mm ± 3 mm
and z = 64 mm ± 3 mm (the 21.01 numbers, your rack must not move them by more than ±3 mm);
every slot 60×60×60 mm ±1 mm with the payload centred.

**21.02-E3.** 20 releases, ≤ 2 jams (a jam in 20 is a slot-tolerance problem — 0.5 mm clearance is
not enough for the balloon; the clay ball should never jam). Landing distance: all payloads
land within 0.3 m of the point directly below the release (the drop height is 0.06-0.1 m —
there is not enough fall to develop spread). The balloon is the troublemaker: it sticks to
the flap on ~1 of 10 releases (static charge) — that is the BOM's "gel-filled (reusable, no
leak)" alt existing.

## Troubleshooting

### Symptom: the payload sticks in the slot after release

Most likely first: clearance (the print shrinks 0.3-0.5 mm — the slot is 0.5 mm too small;
reprint at 100% or file). Then: the payload is wider than the slot at one corner (balloons
are ellipsoids — the slot is a square; add a 45° chamfer). Then: static (the balloon — the
gel alt).

### Symptom: the payload lands behind the robot

The exit is not over the front caster — the payload is leaving the bin at an angle. Check the
flap's hinge side (it must swing *forward*, the hinge is at the back of the slot) and the
bin's front lip (it must be lower than the payload's centre of mass at release, or the
payload tips back).

### Symptom: the LiDAR reports a wall 6 cm ahead, every scan

Payload or rack hardware in the scan plane (z = 0.12 ± 0.02 m). The rack top must stay below
0.10 m — a zip tie loop, a dangling cable or the magnet's printed holder is the usual
culprit. (21.01 Troubleshooting, same failure.)

### Symptom: a "stick" payload that does not stick

The magnet: N52 25×10 mm holds ~2.5 kg against flat steel, but a painted or coated target
halves it; a curved target (a car door) can drop it to a tenth. The glue: UHU sticks in
~30 s — the payload must *arrive* at the target, which a 0.1 m drop from 2 m away does not
guarantee. Both are demo payloads, not precision ones.

## Common mistakes

- Buying payloads before choosing releasers — the releaser determines the slot, the slot
  determines the payload's size. The BOM is organised in that order for a reason.
- Treating the BOM's energy column ("~4 J", "~12 J") as projectile energy — they are stored
  or rated energies; 21.04 converts them.
- Forgetting the LiDAR plane: every new vertical object on the rail is a new wall.
- Letting the magnet onto the deck: it sticks to the chassis, the robot drives into it, and
  the LiDAR sees a 40 cm obstacle that is part of the robot.
- Trusting "it's over the counter" without the date: the CYMA magazine's licence status is
  the law's, and the law's note is dated (BOM: verified 2026-09-24 at Fantom).

## Knowledge check

1. A payload is 0.12 kg. The solenoid pin release needs 8 N net force. Does the payload's
   mass matter for the pin release at all? Why or why not?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Not for the *release* — the pin's job is to withdraw from the payload's sleeve, and 8 N
   beats the spring + friction regardless of the payload's mass. Mass matters for what
   happens AFTER release (the drop: 0.12 kg × 1.4 m/s = 0.12 J) and for the CG. The release
   force budget and the impact energy budget are different budgets.
   </details>

2. Why is the bouncy ball (6 J) more range-critical than the CO2 BB (1.25 J at 100 m/s)?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Because the BB's *ideal* range is 1019 m and drag compresses it to ~51 m, while the
   bouncy ball's ideal range is 24.5 m and drag compresses it to ~11 m. The BB is faster but
   feather-weight (0.25 g) — it is a projectile that the air wins against immediately. The
   bouncy ball is 200× heavier, so it keeps its velocity. Range clearance is set by the
   slower, heavier object.
   </details>

3. The weight switch under slot 2 does not close when you put a balloon in it. Is the slot
   LOADED or EMPTY? What happens if you arm the weapon anyway?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   EMPTY — LOADED is a verified fact (Level 4). Arming anyway and firing is a dry fire:
   the pin ejects, nothing falls, the state machine waits 2 s for a confirmation that never
   comes (the payload-gone check sees the slot still occupied) and declares JAMMED. The
   balloon is the jam — it is in the slot, just not verified. (Or the switch is miswired,
   which 21.09's interlock test matrix catches.)
   </details>

4. A sponsor offers to replace the steel slingshot balls with 8 mm steel balls (0.010 kg).
   What changes in the model, and which lesson's numbers do you have to re-run?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Muzzle velocity drops (same energy, more mass: v = √(2E/m) — 0.60 J into 0.010 kg is
   11.0 m/s instead of 17.4 m/s), impact energy stays 0.60 J (it is the energy that matters
   for safety, and it is unchanged), but the *drag factor* changes (a bigger ball is
   denser relative to its area — the range actually lengthens). Re-run 21.04's slingshot
   numbers and 21.10's range clearance. The eye-protection requirement does not change.
   </details>

5. Why does the BOM put the cold-smoke stick and the sparkler in slots with flame shields,
   while the stink bomb does not need one?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Because the smoke stick burns for 8 s and the sparkler for 2-4 s — the payload is on
   fire while it is still in the bin (the shield protects the bin, the deck and the payload
   next door). The stink bomb's "spray" is a one-second aerosol puff on impact — no
   sustained heat. The shield is a thermal fuse for the bin: it is the difference between
   a payload that burns and a bin that burns.
   </details>

6. Your rack holds 0.6 kg of payload at x = +0.075 m, z = 0.06 m. The Scout's CG must stay
   within ±3 mm of (x = +38 mm, z = 64 mm) or 21.08's numbers are wrong. Does your rack
   keep the Scout inside that box? Show the arithmetic.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>
   Scout base from `war_power_budget.py mass`: 4.16 kg at (x = 38 mm, z = 64 mm) — this
   ALREADY includes 0.45 kg of payload (the model's "payload rack, 3 slots loaded ~0.15 kg
   each" at (0.075, 0.060)). Your 0.6 kg rack is 0.15 kg more than the model at the same
   position: new x = (4.16×0.038 + 0.15×0.075)/4.31 = (0.15808 + 0.01125)/4.31 = 39.3 mm
   (+1.3 mm), new z = (4.16×0.064 + 0.15×0.06)/4.31 = (0.26624 + 0.009)/4.31 = 63.9 mm
   (−0.1 mm). Both inside ±3 mm — but the model's *total mass* is now 4.31 kg, not 4.16 kg,
   so the tipping limits in 21.08 shift slightly. Record it in the spec delta.
   </details>
