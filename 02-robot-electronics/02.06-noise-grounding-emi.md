# 02.06 — Noise, grounding, decoupling and motor EMI

<!-- glance:end -->

## What you will learn

- Trace the return path of every current on your robot, and compute the voltage a shared ground wire adds to your logic levels.
- Lay out a star ground, and say which single wire you would move first on a robot that glitches.
- Place bulk and ceramic capacitors by the **time window** each one covers, instead of by folklore.
- Quantify motor EMI three ways — conducted, magnetic, brush arcing — and choose suppression that doesn't cost you 0.6 W.
- Run an experiment that makes sensor glitches appear and disappear on command, and prove which fix worked.

## Why it matters

There is a class of bug that only exists on real robots: everything works on the bench, and the moment the wheels turn, the ToF sensor reports 8191 mm, the encoder count jumps by 400 ticks, the I²C bus throws `OSError`, or the Pico resets. In simulation this bug does not exist. In a code review it does not exist. It is not in your firmware at all.

It is the 3 A that the H-bridge switches on and off 20,000 times a second, finding its way back to the battery through whatever path you accidentally offered it — which, on a first build, is usually the same thin black wire your sensors use as their zero-volt reference. [02.03](02.03-motor-drivers-in-depth.md) computed the currents. This lesson computes what they do to everything else, and fixes it.

Everything after module 07 assumes clean sensor data. A Kalman filter fed with occasional 400-tick encoder glitches ([10.04](../10-localization/10.04-kalman-filter-1d.md)) does not produce a slightly worse estimate; it produces a confidently wrong one.

## Concept

**Noise is a current that found a return path you didn't plan.**

Every current flows in a loop. You drew the outgoing half of the loop — the red wire — and you probably didn't draw the returning half at all, because you called it "ground" and assumed that all points named ground are the same point. They are not. A wire has inductance (about **1 nH per millimetre**), and an inductance carrying a changing current develops a voltage across it:

$$V = L \frac{di}{dt}$$

The DRV8874 switches 2.95 A in about 150 ns. Along 100 mm of shared ground wire that is:

$$V = 100\ \text{nH} \times \frac{2.95\ \text{A}}{150\ \text{ns}} = 1.97\ \text{V}$$

**Two volts**, twenty thousand times a second, on the wire your Pico calls zero. The Pico's $V_{IL}$ is 0.8 V ([02.05](02.05-logic-levels-and-protection.md)). Every sensor "low" that arrives while that spike is happening is a "high" as far as the Pico is concerned. No amount of software fixes this.

There are exactly three ways the motor's energy reaches your sensors, and the fix is different for each:

| Coupling | Mechanism | What it looks like | Fixed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conducted** (shared impedance) | motor return current flows through a wire that a signal also uses as its reference | glitches proportional to motor *current*; worse on long thin grounds | star ground, thicker/shorter returns |
| **Magnetic** (inductive) | a changing current in one loop induces a voltage in a nearby loop | glitches proportional to $di/dt$; worse on reversals; worse with big loops | twisted pairs, separation, small loop area |
| **Electric / arcing** | brush sparks radiate broadband RF; PWM edges capacitively couple | random, unrelated to load; kills I²C and radio first | capacitors at the motor, shielding, distance |

The .NET analogy that actually holds: a shared ground is a **shared mutable static**. Two subsystems that never reference each other are silently coupled through it, the failures are timing-dependent and non-local, and the fix is not to add locks (filters) but to stop sharing the variable (star ground).

> **Ask your teacher:** "Draw karmel's current loops with the return paths included — motor, regulator, sensors — and show me which two loops share a wire."

## Technical explanation

### Level 1 — Ground bounce, in volts you can compare against a threshold

```bash
python 02-robot-electronics/code/noise_lab.py
```

```text
1) Ground bounce at one PWM edge (edge 150 ns)
    20 mm shared ground wire, 0.50 A step:  0.07 V spike
    20 mm shared ground wire, 2.95 A step:  0.39 V spike
   100 mm shared ground wire, 0.50 A step:  0.33 V spike
   100 mm shared ground wire, 2.95 A step:  1.97 V spike
   300 mm shared ground wire, 0.50 A step:  1.00 V spike
   300 mm shared ground wire, 2.95 A step:  5.90 V spike
```

Compare each number with 0.8 V ($V_{IL}$) and with 0.3 V (the $NM_L$ that [02.01](02.01-datasheets-and-pinouts.md) computed for the Pico→DRV8874 link):

- 20 mm of shared ground at normal current: **0.07 V**, invisible. This is what a soldered board with a ground plane gives you.
- 100 mm — one Dupont jumper across a breadboard — at stall current: **1.97 V**. Guaranteed corruption.
- 300 mm — a "ground wire to the other end of the chassis" — **5.90 V**. This is why the robot resets.

Two design rules fall straight out of it:

1. **Length is the variable you control.** Halving the shared length halves the noise; the current and the edge rate are set by the motor and the driver.
2. **"Shared" is the word that matters, not "ground".** A 300 mm ground wire that no motor current flows through contributes nothing.

**The star.** Make one physical point the reference — a screw terminal, a solder island, a copper pour — and give each subsystem its own return wire to that point:

```text
   WRONG (daisy chain): motor current flows through the sensors' reference
        battery− ──── driver GND ──── Pico GND ──── sensor GND
                 ↑ 3 A here        ↑ and the same wire is the sensors' zero

   RIGHT (star): each return has its own path to one point
                        ┌── driver GND (thick, short)   ← 3 A lives here
        battery− ── ★ ──┼── regulator GND (thick)       ← 2 A here
                        └── Pico + sensor GND (thin)    ← 50 mA here, undisturbed
```

The "one wire beginners get wrong" from [01.07](../01-first-robot/01.07-power-system.md) is the daisy chain. It is not wrong because of resistance — 100 mm of 22 AWG is 5 mΩ, worth 15 mV at 3 A. It is wrong because of **inductance at the switching edge**, which is 130 times larger.

### Level 2 — Decoupling: each capacitor owns a time window

A capacitor near a chip is not "for noise". It is a local energy store that supplies current the wires are too slow to deliver. Which capacitor matters depends entirely on **how long the demand lasts**:

```text
2) A capacitor alone supplying a 2.95 A load step: dip after 150 ns (edge), 25 us (half PWM period), 1 ms
   100 nF ceramic, ESR 0.01 ohm                  :    4.454 V     empty       empty
   10 uF ceramic, ESR 0.005 ohm                  :    0.059 V     7.390 V     empty
   470 uF electrolytic, ESR 0.10 ohm (estimate)  :    0.296 V     0.452 V     6.572 V
   -> ceramics at the pins for the edges, the electrolytic for the PWM period, the battery for the rest
```

Read it as a relay race. ("empty" means the capacitor would be completely discharged before the interval ends.)

| Time scale | Who supplies the current | Why the others can't |
|---|---|---|
| < 1 µs (a switching edge) | **100 nF ceramic** at the chip's own pins | the electrolytic is 50 mm of wire away — 50 nH — and cannot change its current that fast |
| 1 µs – 1 ms (a PWM period, a startup surge) | **470 µF electrolytic** on the driver's VIN | the ceramic is empty in a microsecond |
| > 1 ms (acceleration, stall) | the **battery** | the electrolytic is empty; this is the [02.02](02.02-power-budget.md) sag problem |

The 100 nF row looks like a failure — 4.45 V of droop! — and it is the right answer to the wrong question. A 100 nF doesn't supply 2.95 A; it supplies the tens of milliamps the *chip's gate drivers* need during the edge, and it must be physically at the pins because a centimetre of trace defeats it. Both capacitors are necessary and neither substitutes for the other. The ESR column matters too: at 2.95 A the electrolytic's 0.1 Ω ESR alone costs 0.295 V, which is most of its 150 ns number.

> [!NOTE]
> Capacitors, ESR and flyback from zero → [FE.18 Capacitors and diodes](../optional-foundations/electronics/FE.18-capacitors-diodes.md). What "ground" even means → [FE.16 Grounding](../optional-foundations/electronics/FE.16-grounding.md).

### Level 3 — Motor EMI, and the capacitor advice that is half wrong

A brushed DC motor is a spark generator. The brushes break contact with each commutator segment hundreds of times per second while carrying current through an inductor, and each break is a small arc with nanosecond edges and broadband emission. The standard advice — "solder three 100 nF ceramics on the motor: one across the terminals and one from each terminal to the case" — comes from the era of DC drives and linear supplies. With a 20 kHz H-bridge, the terminal capacitor is no longer free:

```text
3) Capacitor across the motor terminals (12 V, 20 kHz)
      10 nF: extra switching loss   28.8 mW; impedance at 1 MHz  15.92 ohm (motor winding 12566 ohm)
      47 nF: extra switching loss  135.4 mW; impedance at 1 MHz   3.39 ohm (motor winding 12566 ohm)
     100 nF: extra switching loss  288.0 mW; impedance at 1 MHz   1.59 ohm (motor winding 12566 ohm)
     220 nF: extra switching loss  633.6 mW; impedance at 1 MHz   0.72 ohm (motor winding 12566 ohm)
```

Every PWM cycle the bridge charges that capacitor to 12 V and discharges it, and $C V^2 f$ of energy is dissipated in the FETs and the wiring. At 100 nF that is **288 mW per motor, continuously, doing nothing useful** — comparable to the 300 mW the whole Pico consumes, and it lands in the driver whose thermal budget [02.03](02.03-motor-drivers-in-depth.md) showed is already tight.

The practical compromise for a PWM-driven brushed motor:

- **Terminal-to-terminal: 10 nF, not 100 nF.** At 1 MHz (where brush arcing lives) 10 nF is already 16 Ω against the winding's 12.6 kΩ — it shunts almost all of the RF — while costing 29 mW.
- **Terminal-to-case: 10 nF each, if the case is bonded to the chassis ground.** These catch common-mode arcing current and don't sit across the bridge, so they cost nothing in switching loss. Many motors (including the Yahboom 520) already have internal capacitors; check with a capacitance meter before adding more.
- Use **X7R or C0G ceramics rated ≥ 50 V**. A 16 V part across a 12.6 V rail with switching transients is a failure waiting to happen, and Y5V parts lose most of their capacitance at working voltage.

### Level 4 — Magnetic coupling and the case for twisting

A signal pair alongside a motor wire forms a transformer whose mutual inductance depends on the **loop area** the signal pair encloses and its distance from the aggressor:

$$M \approx \frac{\mu_0 \ell}{2\pi} \ln\!\left(\frac{d + w}{d}\right) \qquad V_{induced} = M \frac{di}{dt}$$

```text
4) Coupling from a motor supply wire (2 A step in 150 ns) into a signal pair 20 cm alongside
   untwisted pair, 5 mm apart, 5 mm from motor wire    : M = 27.73 nH, induced 0.370 V
   same, 30 mm from motor wire                         : M =  6.17 nH, induced 0.082 V
   tight twisted pair (residual loop ~0.5 mm), 5 mm away: M =  3.81 nH, induced 0.051 V
```

Three interventions, in order of cost:

1. **Twist the pair.** Free, takes ten seconds, and it is worth a factor of **7** (0.370 V → 0.051 V) — better than moving the wire 25 mm away. Twisting works because it shrinks the enclosed loop area *and* alternates its sign, so successive twists cancel. Twist signal-with-its-own-return: encoder A with encoder ground, SDA with SCL is acceptable but SDA with a ground wire is better.
2. **Separate.** 5 mm → 30 mm is worth a factor of 4.5, and costs chassis space you may not have.
3. **Route motor wires as a pair too.** The aggressor's own loop area matters as much as the victim's. Two motor wires twisted together radiate far less than two wires that take different routes around the chassis.

0.37 V matters: it is more than the 0.30 V of low-level noise margin at the driver's inputs, and it is half the Pico's 0.8 V $V_{IL}$.

### Level 5 — Filtering signals without eating them

If you cannot make the noise smaller, you can make the receiver slower — but only if you know how fast the real signal is. karmel's encoders at full speed:

```text
5) Encoder: at 205 RPM the A/B edges are 119 us apart
     1000 ohm +    1 nF: tau    1.0 us (  0.8 % of edge spacing), a 3.3 V, 20 ns spike leaves   65.3 mV
     1000 ohm +   10 nF: tau   10.0 us (  8.4 % of edge spacing), a 3.3 V, 20 ns spike leaves    6.6 mV
    10000 ohm +   10 nF: tau  100.0 us ( 84.3 % of edge spacing), a 3.3 V, 20 ns spike leaves    0.7 mV
```

The middle row is the design: **1 kΩ + 10 nF** on each encoder channel. A 20 ns, 3.3 V coupled spike arrives at the pin as 6.6 mV — utterly harmless — while the filter's 10 µs time constant is 8 % of the shortest real edge spacing, so quadrature decoding is unaffected. The last row over-filters: at 84 % of the edge spacing, the filter starts eating real transitions at top speed, and you lose counts exactly when the robot is moving fastest, which is the worst possible failure mode for odometry.

The same resistor also does the [02.05](02.05-logic-levels-and-protection.md) job of limiting fault current. One component, two purposes.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Version-sensitive** (verified 2026-09 against TI DRV8874 SLVSF66A for the 150 ns edge and 2.95 A trip point, and the RP2350 datasheet for $V_{IL}$ = 0.8 V).
> The 1 nH/mm wire inductance, the 0.7 pF/cm jumper capacitance, the 0.1 Ω electrolytic ESR and the 2 mH motor inductance are **engineering estimates**, not datasheet values. They are the right order of magnitude and they are not precise. Where a decision depends on one, measure.

## Code

[`02-robot-electronics/code/pico/noise_probe.py`](code/pico/noise_probe.py) is the experiment: it samples the ToF sensor, the INA219, and a **grounded ADC channel** (a 10 kΩ resistor from GP28 to AGND, which should read 0 mV and instead reads your ground noise) under three conditions — motors off, motors at 50 %, motors reversing every 0.4 s.

```python
def sample(condition, i2c, tof, ina, adc, emit):
    """Read every signal once and emit 'condition,signal,value' lines; I2C failures emit 'err'."""
    try:
        mm = tof.read_mm(timeout_ms=150)
        emit("%s,tof_mm,%s" % (condition, "err" if mm is None else mm))
    except OSError:
        emit("%s,tof_mm,err" % condition)
    ...
    emit("%s,adc_gnd_mv,%.1f" % (condition, adc.read_u16() * 3300 / 65535))
```

The point of the design is that **the sensor is looking at a wall that does not move**. Every difference between the three conditions is noise, and `noise_lab.analyse()` quantifies it with a median-absolute-deviation outlier count, which is robust to exactly the kind of one-sample spike you are hunting.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Safety:** `noise_probe.py` spins both motors at 50–60 % duty and **reverses them every 0.4 s**, which is the harshest normal electrical event your robot ever produces. Robot on a stand, **wheels in the air**, hand on the battery switch, nothing loose nearby, no cables where a wheel can catch them ([SAFETY.md](../SAFETY.md) rules 1, 2, 7). Ctrl-C stops it; the `finally` block brakes both motors.

## Expected result

- **02.06-E1:** the grounded ADC channel degrades most in *relative* terms (it should read 0 and reads tens of millivolts); the ToF degrades most in *consequence*. Expect **both** kinds of ToF failure: `err` (a NAK, meaning the bus transaction itself was corrupted) and wrong-but-plausible numbers (meaning the sensor's own analog front end or its data bits were disturbed). `reverse` is worse than `run50`, because a reversal changes the current in the motor inductance as fast as the bridge allows and at the largest possible amplitude, while steady 50 % duty is a repetitive, smaller step. Perfect grounding gives the ADC channel a few millivolts of ADC offset and nothing else; imperfect grounding gives tens to hundreds of millivolts with peaks synchronised to the reversals.
- **02.06-E2:** on a typical breadboard build, `off` gives standard deviations of a few millivolts and a couple of millimetres, zero outliers; `reverse` typically gives 5–50× the standard deviation on the ADC channel and a handful of ToF outliers or errors. Moving the Pico's ground to the driver's GND pin should make it clearly worse — that is the whole point of the exercise, and if it *doesn't*, your wires are so short that inductance isn't your dominant coupling yet (congratulations; twist them anyway). Numerical alternative: 210 mV over 40 nH with a 2 A step needs $dt = L\,di/V = 40\text{n} \times 2 / 0.21 =$ **381 ns**, i.e. a slower edge than the datasheet's 150 ns — so either the coupling path is shorter than 40 mm, or you are seeing the filtered residue of a faster, larger spike. Both conclusions are useful.
- **02.06-E3:** on a first build the star ground usually wins by the largest margin, twisting is second, and the motor capacitors are third. The encoder RC filter often shows **no** improvement in this test (the ToF is on I²C, not on the encoder pins) and still belongs on the robot, because it protects a signal this experiment doesn't measure. If step 3 makes anything worse, check the electrolytic's polarity and its voltage rating. If step 4 makes the driver hotter, that is [02.03](02.03-motor-drivers-in-depth.md)'s $C V^2 f$ and you chose too large a capacitor.
- **02.06-E4:** (1) $\ell = V\,dt/(1\text{nH/mm} \times di) = 0.3 \times 150\text{n}/(10^{-9} \times 2.95) =$ **15 mm**. That is the honest answer, and it is why a soldered board with a short, wide ground exists. (2) At 0.5 A: **90 mm**. (3) At a 1 µs edge: **102 mm** at 2.95 A — six times more room; it costs switching loss ($\tfrac12 V I (t_r+t_f) f$ scales with edge time, so ≈ 6× the switching component, 0.076 W → 0.45 W at 2.1 A) and it is not adjustable on the DRV8874 anyway. (4) With a plane, ≈ **150 mm** at stall current: a plane makes the problem disappear, which is most of why 02.08 exists.
- **02.06-E5:** at 12.6 V and 20 kHz, $CV^2f$ per motor: 10 nF → **31.8 mW**, 47 nF → **149 mW**, 100 nF → **318 mW**; for two motors, 64 mW / 299 mW / 635 mW. Against [02.02](02.02-power-budget.md)'s 9.9 W average that is 0.6 % / 3.0 % / 6.4 %, i.e. roughly 1.3 / 6 / 13 minutes off a 3.3 h (198 min) runtime. At 1 MHz the impedances are 15.9 Ω, 3.4 Ω and 1.6 Ω against the winding's ~12.6 kΩ, so even 10 nF shunts over 99.8 % of the arcing current. **10 nF is the right answer**: it gets essentially all of the RF benefit for a twentieth of the loss, and the marginal benefit of 100 nF is unmeasurable while its cost is 318 mW in the driver you are already thermally limited by.
- **02.06-E6:** (1) 333 RPM ≈ 34.9 rad/s at the motor's output... at the wheel, edges come $4 \times 11 \times 30$ = 1320 per wheel revolution at 333/60 = 5.55 rev/s → **7326 edges/s**, spacing **137 µs**. (Slower gearing means *fewer* teeth per wheel turn but a faster wheel; the two nearly cancel, which is worth noticing.) (2) τ ≤ 13.7 µs; 1 kΩ + 10 nF (τ = 10 µs) still fits, and leaves a 20 ns spike at 6.6 mV — it already meets the requirement. (3) 1 kΩ at a 12 V fault allows (12 − 3.8)/1000 = **8.2 mA**, which is too much: use 4.7 kΩ + 2.2 nF (τ = 10.3 µs, fault 1.7 mA) if you want both. (4) The original filter stays safe as long as τ ≤ 10 % of the spacing, i.e. spacing ≥ 100 µs, i.e. ≤ 10,000 edges/s, i.e. a wheel speed of $10000 \times 2\pi / 2464$ = **25.5 rad/s** — about 19 % above the motor's no-load 21.5 rad/s and 50 % above `max_wheel_speed_rad_s: 17.0`. There is margin, but not a lot, which is exactly why the value was chosen from the real number rather than by habit.

## Troubleshooting

### Symptom: sensors glitch only when the motors run

This is the lesson's canonical bug. Work it in this order, because each step is cheaper than the next:

1. **Confirm the correlation numerically.** Run `noise_probe.py`. If `off` and `run50` are identical and `reverse` is bad, it is $di/dt$ (magnetic or ground bounce). If `run50` is already bad in proportion to current, it is conducted (shared ground).
2. **Find the shared wire.** Trace the motor current's *return* path with a finger on the schematic. Any wire that carries motor return current and is also referenced by a sensor is the bug. Move it to the star point.
3. **Twist.** Motor pairs, encoder pairs. Ten seconds, factor of seven.
4. **Check the capacitors.** 470 µF at the driver's VIN, 100 nF at its pins, correct polarity, ≥ 25 V rating.
5. **Separate physically.** Get sensor wiring off the motor wiring; cross at right angles where they must meet, never run parallel.
6. **Only then filter.** RC on the digital lines, sized from Level 5. Filtering first hides the diagnosis and keeps the noise in the system, where it will reappear as something else.

### Symptom: the Pico resets when the motors start

Not noise — that is brownout, and it belongs to [02.02](02.02-power-budget.md). Measure the 5 V and 3.3 V rails with `brownout_sim.py`'s model in mind. If the rails are solid and it still resets, suspect a ground-bounce-induced reset on the RUN pin: add a 100 nF from RUN to ground.

### Symptom: the ADC reads a sensible value that is offset by tens of millivolts

The ADC measures against **its** ground. If the ADC's ground reference carries any motor return current, the offset is $I \times R$ of that path, plus a switching-synchronous component. Fixes: use Pico pin 33 (AGND) as the reference for every analog ground, keep the analog ground's connection to the star short, and read the grounded-channel value as a live measurement of your own ground quality (that is exactly what `noise_probe.py`'s `adc_gnd_mv` is for).

### Symptom: encoder counts jump by hundreds

1. Are the encoder wires twisted with their ground and away from motor wires?
2. Is the encoder supply 3.3 V from the Pico, with a 100 nF at the motor's encoder connector?
3. Fit 1 kΩ + 10 nF on each channel (Level 5) and re-measure.
4. If the jumps are *bidirectional* and happen at reversals, it is noise. If they accumulate in one direction at high speed, it is missed edges — a different bug ([01.09](../01-first-robot/01.09-reading-encoders.md)).

### Symptom: a fix made it worse

Usually one of three: an electrolytic fitted backwards; a capacitor across the motor that is large enough to make the driver hotter and slower; or an added ground wire that created a **loop** (two paths between the same two points) rather than a star branch. Remove the last change and re-measure — one change at a time is not a style preference here, it is the only way the experiment means anything.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "ground" as one node.** It is a network of wires with inductance, and two points on it differ by volts during switching edges.
- **Daisy-chaining grounds** because the wire was already there. Motor current then flows through the sensors' reference.
- **Adding filters before finding the coupling path.** Filters raise the noise floor you can tolerate; they don't remove the energy, and it will surface somewhere you haven't filtered.
- **Fitting 100 nF across motor terminals by reflex.** 288 mW per motor at 20 kHz, in the part that is already your thermal limit.
- **Using a 16 V electrolytic on a 12.6 V bus.** Switching transients and bus pumping ([02.03](02.03-motor-drivers-in-depth.md)) exceed it. 25 V minimum.
- **Putting the bulk capacitor at the battery instead of at the driver.** Its job is to be *electrically close* to the load; 30 cm of wire is 300 nH between it and where it is needed.
- **Changing three things at once**, then not knowing which one worked — and carrying two useless modifications forever.
- **Believing that a problem which disappears is solved.** If you didn't measure it before and after, you moved a wire and got lucky.

## Knowledge check

1. Why does a shared ground wire matter far more for its inductance than for its resistance?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   At 3 A, 100 mm of 22 AWG (≈ 5 mΩ) drops about 15 mV — negligible. The same wire has about 100 nH, and the DRV8874 changes 2.95 A in 150 ns, so $V = L\,di/dt$ = 1.97 V. Resistance responds to the *value* of the current, inductance to its *rate of change*, and a switching H-bridge is all rate of change.
   </details>

2. You have a 100 nF ceramic at the driver's pins and a 470 µF electrolytic 40 mm away. A 2.95 A load step lasts 25 µs. Who supplies it, and what would happen with only one of them fitted?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For the first fraction of a microsecond the ceramic does, because the electrolytic is 40 nH of wire away and cannot change its current fast enough. From then on the electrolytic supplies it and dips about 0.45 V. With only the ceramic, it empties in about a microsecond and the rail collapses at each edge. With only the electrolytic, the edges are unsupported and you get ringing and radiated noise. They cover different time windows and neither substitutes for the other.
   </details>

3. Compute the induced voltage in a 20 cm untwisted signal pair (5 mm spacing) running 5 mm from a motor wire carrying a 2 A step in 150 ns, and compare it with the Pico's $V_{IL}$.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $M = \frac{\mu_0 \times 0.20}{2\pi}\ln\frac{5+5}{5}$ = 27.7 nH; $V = M\,di/dt = 27.7\text{n} \times 2/150\text{n}$ = **0.37 V**. That is 46 % of the 0.8 V $V_{IL}$ and more than the 0.30 V low-level noise margin at the DRV8874's inputs. Twisting the pair to a 0.5 mm residual loop cuts it to 0.051 V.
   </details>

4. Predict: you solder 100 nF across each motor's terminals to reduce brush noise. What happens to the driver's temperature, and by how much?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Each capacitor is charged and discharged by the bridge every PWM cycle, dissipating $CV^2f$ = 100 nF × 12.6² × 20 kHz = **318 mW** per motor in the FETs and the wiring. On a carrier at ≈ 70 °C/W that alone is over 20 °C of junction temperature, on top of the conduction loss — enough to move you meaningfully toward the 160 °C thermal shutdown under load. 10 nF gives essentially the same RF attenuation for 32 mW.
   </details>

5. Debugging: `noise_probe.py` shows identical statistics for `off` and `run50`, but four ToF outliers and three I²C errors during `reverse`. What kind of coupling is it, and what do you change first?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Steady current doesn't hurt, changing current does: it is $di/dt$-driven, i.e. magnetic coupling or ground bounce, not resistive shared-impedance coupling. Change the cheapest high-leverage thing first: give the Pico its own ground return to the star point, and twist the motor pairs. Re-measure after each. Adding an RC filter would be third at best, and adding it first would hide which of the two it was.
   </details>

6. Why does twisting a pair help more than moving it 25 mm away from the aggressor?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Induced voltage is proportional to the loop area the victim encloses. Moving away reduces the field strength roughly logarithmically (0.370 V → 0.082 V for 5 mm → 30 mm, a factor of 4.5). Twisting reduces the loop area to almost nothing *and* alternates its orientation so successive loops induce opposite voltages that cancel (0.370 V → 0.051 V, a factor of 7). It is also free and needs no chassis space.
   </details>

7. You fit a 10 kΩ + 10 nF filter on the encoder channels and your odometry gets worse at high speed but better at low speed. Explain.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   τ = 100 µs, which is 84 % of the 119 µs between encoder edges at karmel's top speed. At low speed the edges are far apart and the filter only removes noise; at high speed the filter cannot follow the real edges, so transitions are missed or merged and counts are lost. The filter must be sized against the fastest real signal: 1 kΩ + 10 nF (τ = 10 µs, 8 % of the spacing) is the correct choice.
   </details>

8. Your robot works perfectly on the bench with a bench power supply and glitches on battery. What changed electrically?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Several things, and all of them are this lesson: a bench supply has large output capacitance and a low-impedance connection, so it absorbs the switching currents that the battery's wiring inductance turns into voltage; the bench supply's ground is usually a single short lead to one point rather than a daisy chain around a chassis; and battery wiring is longer and routed next to motor wiring. Measure the bulk capacitance and the star quality, not the battery.
   </details>

## Practical challenge

Turn "my robot glitches sometimes" into a **measured before/after report**, and make the after-state permanent.

1. Capture `noise_before.csv` on your robot exactly as built, with the ground-noise ADC channel wired.
2. Apply the five interventions of E3 **one at a time**, capturing a CSV after each, and produce a table of `reverse / tof_mm` outliers, `tof_mm_errors`, and `adc_gnd_mv` standard deviation across the six runs.
3. Pick the smallest set of changes that reaches the target and keep only those. Photograph the wiring and write down which wire goes to the star point.
4. Run a 10-minute drive on the floor with the winning configuration, logging the same signals, and confirm the numbers hold outside the stand.

**Acceptance criteria:** a six-row before/after table with real counts; `reverse` statistics for `tof_mm` and `adc_gnd_mv` within **2×** of the `off` statistics, and **zero** `tof_mm_errors` over 60 samples; a named winning change with its measured effect; a photo or sketch of your star point showing every return wire; and a 10-minute floor run with no I²C errors logged.
